Nopiate

A private taper companion for 7-OH, with an AI copilot that reads how you're doing and paces the taper around it. Two visual directions below, then the system surfaces (Live Activity, widgets, Apple Watch) that make logging effortless. Numbers throughout are your real ones: baseline ~45 mg/day, three half-tablets a day now.

Log a dose

The wave. For the taper's rough patches. Your own words from onboarding, and a no-shame exit: "Log a dose" sits right there, neutral. Note the subtitle — the popular "cravings pass in 20 minutes" line turns out to be unsourced folklore, so the app measures your urges from start/end timestamps and quotes your own average back to you. Honest and more persuasive.

  • Amber, never red. Going over plan renders the ring and chip in muted amber with copy like "18 mg today, plan was 15." No red screens, no exclamation marks — logging an over-plan dose looks identical to logging any dose.
  • Step context lives in the date line. "Step 6" quietly anchors today inside the larger plan without making the plan the point of every glance.
  • Evening window nudge. Because your biggest historical dose was evening (20 mg at baseline), the evening slot can open a few minutes later each week — stretching the interval is a second taper axis alongside mg.

Trends. Every number only accumulates or averages — nothing here can reset. The daily withdrawal check-in (a shortened SOWS scale) is what tells the taper engine when to suggest a hold before you'd even ask.

  • The withdrawal check-in is load-bearing. A 10-item SOWS-lite each evening (10 taps, under 20 seconds) gives the app the signal it needs: rising scores across 2–3 days → the Plan screen proactively offers "Hold this step?" That's the just-in-time intervention this profile actually needs — you said cravings only bite when withdrawal does.
  • Serif numerals are the identity. New York for numbers, SF for controls. It reads as a document you keep, not a game you play.
  • The amber dot convention carries across both directions: off-plan moments are recorded in amber ink, styled exactly like every other data point.
An honest note about that finish date. My first draft of this screen said "done around Oct 30." That was wrong, and the correction matters more than any pixel on this page. At 15 mg you're already at about a third of your baseline, which is exactly where dose-to-receptor-occupancy stops being linear — each equal-milligram cut from here removes a much bigger share of occupancy than the same cut did at 45 mg. The evidence-based rate below the 30% mark is 1–5% of your current dose every 1–2 weeks, which puts the real finish somewhere around February 2027, roughly 26 more steps.

That sounds discouraging and it is the opposite. Tapers running 12–52 weeks are about 3.6× more likely to finish than short ones, and tapers over a year about 6.7×. Reductions slower than 5% per week have significantly better outcomes than faster ones. The intuition that a fast taper is the decisive, serious one is backwards — slow is the version that works. An app that quietly shows you an optimistic date is setting you up to feel behind; this one should show the real one and make the long timeline feel like the plan rather than a sentence.

Constraints from the pharmacology

Three things the drug dictates

These came out of the research and they change the product, not just the copy. Each one is a rule the taper engine has to enforce whether or not it's visible on screen.

  • Cuts lie for two days. 7-OH has a ~4-hour half-life on any single dose but accumulates to a >20-hour effective half-life in daily users. A reduction can feel completely fine for 48 hours and then bite on day three. Design consequence: the app must never offer "you're doing great, want to cut early?" on 24 hours of good data. Step-down prompts are gated on a minimum 5–7 day hold and a flat-or-falling withdrawal trend.
  • Percent of current, not percent of baseline. Community taper logs that cut 10% of the original dose every two days visibly fall apart around day four. The rate that holds is ~10% of the current dose every 7–14 days — so your steps get smaller in absolute mg as you descend: 15 → 13.5 → 12 → 11, and near the end 6 → 5.5 → 5. At your level each step is about half a 5 mg tablet, which is why the plan should switch you to weighing crushed tablet powder below ~7 mg/day.
  • A brand switch is a hidden dose change. Lab analysis of commercial 7-OH products found actual content running 32–81% of the labeled amount, with 10–17% variation tablet-to-tablet within the same bottle. Switching from an under-dosed brand to an accurate one at the "same mg" can be a real 2–3× jump; the reverse reads as unexplained withdrawal and looks like taper failure. Design consequence: log brand + lot with every dose, flag any change loudly, and treat the entire taper as percent-of-your-own-baseline rather than trusting absolute milligrams.
Two safety items the app should carry, quietly but permanently. First: tolerance falls during a taper, which makes a relapse at your old 40–50 mg dose the classic overdose scenario — naloxone on hand is cheap insurance, and it reverses 7-OH. Second: federal scheduling of concentrated 7-OH is in motion right now, so supply could vanish mid-taper and force a cold turkey you didn't choose. Worth having a fallback settled before the plan reaches its middle. Neither of these needs to be a screen — a single line in a "Just in case" section is enough.

Where I'd land

Recommendation: Tidewater as the app's skin, Ledger's chart language inside it. The Today, Log, and Wave screens want Tidewater's dark calm — they're used morning and night, often in bed. The Plan and Trends screens want Ledger's staircase chart and serif numerals — but they can live on the dark ground with the same moss-to-teal ink. One app, two registers: calm surface, rigorous ledger underneath.

One naming note: Nopiate is a good project name and a poor springboard label — it reads as drug-related to anyone glancing at your phone, which cuts against the privacy posture the rest of the design takes seriously. The bundle can stay Nopiate while the home-screen name and lock-screen strings are something flat like "Waypoint" or "Daily."

Since these mockups were drawn

What changed

Your product list and the copilot request both moved the design. These screens don't reflect the changes yet — the next pass will.

  • The dose numbers on these screens are fiction, and that's the finding. Five products at 10, 11, 12, 13, and 18 mg, halved by hand, means "15 mg/day" is only true on all-Omega days — the honest nominal range is 15 to 27 mg/day depending on which bottle is open. Layer in label inaccuracy and the real intake spans roughly 5 to 22 mg. A 5% taper step is 0.75 mg. The step is smaller than the noise, so the Plan screen's tidy staircase is currently drawing a line through static.
  • The Astral Glitter powder moves to the front of the plan. It was slated for the endgame below 7 mg; it should come first, because it's what turns the baseline into a real number. Volumetric dosing — weigh ~100 mg where the scale is accurate, dissolve to 1 mg/mL, dose by oral syringe — gives 0.25 mg resolution, far finer than any step this taper needs.
  • The log sheet's "batch" row becomes a preparation row. Tablet + brand + lot during crossover, then solution + batch date + concentration afterward. Same purpose: never let a silent potency change read as a taper signal.
  • The evening SOWS form is replaced by free text. Sixteen items a night is the burden that kills logging adherence. You describe how you feel, whenever you want, typed or spoken; the on-device model scores it onto the same 16 items. The real questionnaire runs occasionally to keep the model honest.
  • The copilot needs a home in the navigation. Current tabs are Today / Plan / Trends / Toolkit. It probably replaces Toolkit — an always-available conversation that happens to contain the toolkit, rather than a menu of tools sitting next to a separate chat.
  • Adjuncts join the log. Cannabis, melatonin, magnesium, and the rest go in the same intake stream so the app can answer "how bad was the RLS on magnesium nights versus without" from your own data — which, given how thin the supplement literature is, is better evidence than anything I could quote you.
